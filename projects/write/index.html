Problem + Requirements

A key challenge in Tax Advice AI was workflow fragmentation — users spent more effort navigating the process than applying professional judgement.

→ Inputs
• Case details without clear starting structure
• Knowledge and legislation disconnected from drafting
Outputs →
• Drafts requiring repeated rewrites
• Low confidence in review readiness
Interaction
• Editing without intent awareness
• Manual source-to-content mapping

🙇‍♀️Starting From Uncertainty

When creating a new tax document, users are immediately forced to make decisions without enough guidance. Choosing between a blank document or a precedent-based draft requires upfront knowledge of structure, scope, and expectations—creating hesitation before any real work can begin.

🤷Context That Feels Optional

Although knowledge bases are meant to provide critical context, users struggle to understand when and how they should be used. Without clear signals of impact, knowledge bases feel disconnected from the drafting workflow and are often skipped or underutilised.

🤦‍♂️Too Much to Verify, Too Little to Act On

When reviewing generated content, users are presented with extensive legislation and source references. While comprehensive, the information is difficult to prioritise and cannot be directly acted upon, forcing users to mentally connect sources back to the draft.

🙂‍↕️Editing Without Intent

Users can edit or rewrite content, but the system treats all changes as generic text updates. Without understanding the user’s intent—such as improving technical accuracy or adjusting tone—editing becomes an iterative trial-and-error process rather than a guided refinement.

Solutions

To simplify how users begin, I designed a single, flexible prompt input on the homepage that supports a wide range of drafting needs without requiring upfront decisions about structure or format.

Inside the editor, an intelligent agent sidebar provides continuous guidance and refinement, while a clean, structured layout ensures generated content remains clear, scannable, and easy to review—keeping complex tax advice readable rather than overwhelming.

Unified Input

A single, flexible prompt input acts as the primary entry point, allowing users to start drafting without deciding structure, format, or workflow upfront.

Agent Sidebar

An intelligent agent sidebar provides continuous guidance, context, and refinement actions, enabling users to research, reason, and revise without leaving the editor.

Clear Output

A clean, structured document layout prioritises readability and hierarchy, ensuring complex tax content remains scannable, review-ready, and easy to validate.

👉From blank starts to guided intent

Users no longer begin with an empty page. A single, universal prompt box brings together files, knowledge bases, and recommended prompts—helping them move from intent to draft without figuring out where to start.

😊Knowledge appears when it’s needed

Instead of switching tools to verify sources, legislative context and precedents surface alongside the content. Users stay focused on drafting while the system quietly pulls in the right references at the right moment.

✨Edit with clarity, not clutter

The editor keeps the document front and center, while the AI assistant works in the background. Changes, rewrites, and assumptions are clearly separated, reducing cognitive load and making revisions easier to trust.

👀Every insight stays accountable

Inline citations, source panels, and version history ensure users always know why something was written. The design turns AI assistance into a transparent collaborator—supporting confidence, not replacing judgment.

Feedback

User and stakeholder feedback surfaced overlooked scenarios such as live collaboration, shared prompts, prompt history, and dark mode. These perspectives expanded the experience beyond my initial assumptions and strengthened the overall product direction.

Technical Constraints

Although technically viable, certain features — such as embedding a full AI prompt system on the homepage — required phased implementation due to complexity. Engineering feedback led to feature decomposition and alternative solutions, shaping the next design iteration.

Alternative distributed intelligence

When a centralized prompt input wasn’t feasible, intelligence was distributed across prediction, templates, and automated prefill — preserving automation while reducing user effort.

Share & Collaborate

Collaboration and shared knowledge transform drafting from an individual task into a connected workflow — aligning people, context, and AI reasoning within a single system.

Prompt history

AI interactions are treated as persistent, searchable artefacts — enabling transparent comparison and continuous refinement.

Key takeaways from the journey

Alignment is messy — but clarity is earned

Long Geng

PwC AI CoE

If this project taught me anything, it’s that alignment doesn’t happen naturally — it has to be worked through.

Tax Advice/Write wasn’t a clean, linear progression from idea to solution. There were disagreements about scope, debates over whether we were over-engineering certain flows, and moments where technical constraints forced us to rethink features we had already invested time in. More than once, we had to pause and admit that what looked elegant in Figma didn’t hold up in reality.

At times, the tension between user expectations and engineering feasibility felt uncomfortable. Some ideas had to be scaled back. Others were broken into milestones not because it was ideal, but because it was necessary. There were compromises. There were reworks. There were conversations that took longer than expected.

But those frictions were not setbacks — they were clarifiers.

Each tradeoff forced us to define what actually mattered. Was this feature solving a core user pain, or were we polishing something that felt impressive but added little value? Were we building for today’s demo, or for long-term adoption inside PwC’s workflows? Those questions weren’t always easy, but they sharpened our direction.

What changed over time wasn’t just the product — it was our shared understanding. The more we tested, debated, and validated, the more intentional our decisions became. Not perfect. Not always smooth. But grounded.

In the end, the project didn’t succeed because everything went according to plan. It succeeded because we were willing to adjust the plan. We learned to treat constraints as design inputs, feedback as signal rather than criticism, and misalignment as something to resolve — not avoid.

The biggest takeaway for me is this: real product impact rarely comes from frictionless execution. It comes from navigating uncertainty, negotiating tradeoffs, and staying honest about what the problem really is. When clarity is earned — not assumed — the outcome becomes stronger than any initial idea.
